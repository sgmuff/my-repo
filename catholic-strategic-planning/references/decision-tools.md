# Decision Tools (Name and Judge, Stages 4 and 5)

## Decisive questions (Name)

A decisive question is one the plan must answer for the mission to thrive over the plan horizon. Phrase each as a question the organization can act on: "How should we...?" or "Should we...?"

**The four-part test.** A candidate passes only if all four hold:

1. **Consequential.** Getting it wrong would materially harm the mission or the organization's viability within the plan horizon.
2. **Within our power.** The organization, or an authority it can petition, can act on it. A fact of life ("Mass attendance is falling") isn't a question; what to do about it may be.
3. **Genuinely open.** Faithful, informed people could answer it differently, and every answer has a cost.
4. **Grounded.** It traces to findings from See, not to a board member's favorite idea.

**Five categories.** Sort each passing question into one:

- **Mission focus:** what we do and for whom.
- **Identity and fidelity:** how we live as Catholic and keep the charism.
- **Sustainability:** money, property, and other assets.
- **People and leadership:** staff, volunteers, board, succession.
- **Structure and relationships:** partnerships, mergers, sponsorship, and governance.

**Candidates that fail the test.** An operational problem goes on an action list. A mandate goes to compliance. An open factual question goes on "data still owed." Tell the team where each one went, so nothing feels dropped.

Aim for three to seven questions. More than seven usually means operational items slipped through; fewer than three usually means the hard questions are being avoided. If a category has no question, say so and ask whether See looked there; don't invent a question to fill it.

**Framing rules.**

- Don't prejudge the answer. Where a duty is fixed (caring for elderly members, honoring restricted gifts), ask how to fulfill it, not whether.
- A fact isn't a question. "No vocations in eight years" stays evidence; a question about how to respond to it may go forward.
- Don't bundle decisions that different authorities or stakeholders must make. Group them under a theme if that helps the reader, but keep each decision separate.
- Mark any question you suggest that the team's findings didn't raise as your proposal.
- Questions stay drafts until the people most affected have been heard on them. The leadership team's agreement isn't communal discernment.

## Generating options (Judge)

For each decisive question, set out at least three real options, including the strongest version of the option the leader least prefers. Draw from the TOWS candidates. If the leader supplied the options, ask what's missing, and split any option that bundles separate decisions (a services contract and a governance affiliation are different choices; a capital campaign is a way to fund a strategy, not a strategy). For each option, state what it costs: money, people, time, and what gets given up. Don't call an option financially sound, or say it closes a gap, without figures.

Before scoring, ask who on the team stands to gain or lose personally from each option: their job, their program, a family or business tie. Those people may share what they know, but they step out of scoring and the decision on that question, and the planning record notes it. If the person with a stake holds the final authority, say so and suggest how the decision can be reviewed by someone without that stake.

## Criteria matrix

The default weights below are a starting point. Have the team adjust them before scoring, and record the final weights, since the weights are where values hide.

| Criterion | Default weight |
| --- | --- |
| Mission impact | 30 |
| Fidelity to identity and charism | 20 |
| Service to the poor and vulnerable | 15 |
| Feasibility with current or obtainable capacity | 15 |
| Financial sustainability | 15 |
| Risk (higher score means lower risk) | 5 |

Score each option 1 to 5 per criterion, multiply by the weight, and total, computing in code. Label your scores as a provisional reading for the team to rescore. Show the table, and show how the ranking changes if the two largest weights shift. Then say plainly that the totals are an input to discernment, not the decision.

## CST screen

Run this on every option that survives the matrix, including options you added. It is a screen, kept separate from the weights, so a strong financial case can't quietly outvote a moral concern.

Score each principle from -2 (works against it) to +2 (advances it), with a one-line reason:

1. **Human dignity:** does the option treat every person affected as an end, never a means?
2. **Common good:** does it serve the wider community, not only the organization?
3. **Subsidiarity:** does it respect and strengthen people and groups closer to the need?
4. **Solidarity:** does it bind the organization to those it serves rather than holding them at arm's length?
5. **Preferential option for the poor:** what happens to the poorest people affected?
6. **Care for creation:** what is its environmental cost?
7. **Dignity of work:** what does it mean for wages, conditions, and workers' voice?

Rules:

- Any -2 halts the option. Redesign it to remove the harm or drop it.
- Two or more -1 scores require a written mitigation before the option goes forward. Count the scores in code.
- Report the screen alongside the matrix, never folded into it.

## Cooperation and scandal screening

Run this when an option involves a partner, funder, vendor, or affiliate whose activities include something contrary to Church teaching.

1. **Name the wrongful activity** and how the option connects to it.
2. **Formal cooperation:** does the organization share the intention behind the wrong, or does the arrangement itself carry out the wrong? If so, the option is excluded.
3. **Material cooperation:** if not formal, how close is the connection?
   - *Immediate:* the organization's own action is part of the wrongful act. Treat as excluded.
   - *Mediate:* the organization contributes something that the partner uses elsewhere. Weigh how proximate the contribution is, whether it is necessary to the wrong, the gravity of the wrong, and the good at stake.
4. **Scandal:** could the arrangement lead others to think the Church approves of the wrong, or lead someone into sin (*Catechism* §§2284 to 2287)? Consider who would see it and how it would be reported.
5. **Specific norms.** For charities, the diocesan bishop is to ensure agencies don't receive support from groups pursuing ends contrary to Church teaching, or accept contributions for initiatives whose ends or means don't conform to it (*Intima Ecclesiae natura* Art. 10 §3). For U.S. health care, apply the ERDs' directives on partnerships.
6. **Safeguards** that would reduce the connection or the scandal: contract terms, separation of funds and activities, public explanation.

Give a preliminary risk reading (lower concern, concern that safeguards may address, serious concern, or unclear) and say what facts would change it. This is an initial assessment, not a moral or canonical determination. **Always refer the final determination** to the diocesan bishop or his delegate, and, where relevant, the sponsor, the major superior, or an ethicist the diocese recognizes. Say what to bring: the agreement draft, a description of the partner's activities, and this analysis.

## Scenario stress test

Standard and Comprehensive tiers only.

1. Pick the two uncertain drivers from the STEEPLE and ecclesial scan with the most effect on the mission.
2. Build two to four plausible futures from them and give each a short name.
3. Test each recommended strategy against each future: robust (works in all), fragile (fails in one or more), or contingent (works only if a condition holds).
4. For fragile strategies, add an early warning sign and a fallback. Feed the warning signs into the off-cycle triggers in `execution-and-review.md`.

## Stop-doing list

Every Judge stage produces at least one decision to stop, shrink, or hand off a program or activity, or a written explanation, with evidence, of why nothing should stop. Resources don't appear from nowhere, and a plan that only adds things is a plan the staff will quietly refuse.

For each item:

- what stops and when;
- what money, time, and people it frees, and where they go. Count only avoidable cost; shared overhead and restricted funding don't disappear with the program;
- **the people affected:** how those served will be told, referred elsewhere, or accompanied through the change. Whenever a closure is on the table, even on hold, sketch the transition: records transfer, continuity for people with ongoing needs, a timeline, and an owner. Ending a service is also an act toward real people, and it should be done with the same care as starting one.
