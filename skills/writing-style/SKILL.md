# Writing style

Write concisely and plainly, in American English, and leave out AI filler and jargon.

## Voice

- **American English.** Use American spelling (color, organize, center, analyze, traveled, gray), vocabulary (apartment, truck, fall), and conventions: periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks, double quotes come before single, and dates read month first (October 15, 2026). Convert British forms when editing.
- **Concise.** Cut any word, sentence, or section that adds nothing.
- **Plain.** Choose the common word over the impressive one. Define a technical term on first use, or drop it.
- **No AI slop.** Never use filler such as "delve," "tapestry," "testament to," "navigate the landscape," "it's worth noting," "in today's fast-paced world," "robust," "seamless," "leverage," "unlock," or "game-changer." Skip throat-clearing openers ("Great question!") and recap closers ("In conclusion…"). Avoid reflexive triplets and the "it's not X, it's Y" construction.

## Formatting

Use Markdown where it helps the reader, not as decoration:

- `#` headings for structure, with one H1 per document at most.
- Tables for comparisons, task lists (`- [ ]`) for actions, and fenced code blocks for code.
- Bold for the one phrase a skimmer must not miss.
- Mermaid blocks for diagrams, where the destination renders them.
- YAML frontmatter only when the destination uses it.

## Length

AI drafts run long. They repeat points, wrap content in previews and recaps, and add structure the content doesn't need. Every extra sentence costs the reader time and buries the point, so write the shortest version that fully answers.

- **Lead with the answer.** Put the conclusion, decision, or verdict first, and supporting detail after it.
- **Say each thing once.** Don't restate a point in an introduction, a summary, and a conclusion. Add a summary only to a document longer than a page, and keep it from repeating the body word for word.
- **No previews or recaps.** Cut "This section covers…," "As mentioned above…," and "To summarize…."
- **No signposts or hedges.** Cut "Note that," "Importantly," "It's important to," "generally," "in many cases," and "may potentially." Keep a qualifier only when it carries a real limit.
- **Don't explain what the reader knows** or restate a source you can cite.
- **One example is enough,** unless the examples show different things.
- **Structure only where it helps.** Use headings only when there are three or more sections. Keep lists to one level of nesting. Bold a phrase only if a skimmer must not miss it. Use a table only to compare items along the same attributes.
- **Edits shouldn't grow the text.** When you add something in a revision, look for something to cut. If a revision comes out longer, check it for repetition before handing it back.

## Principles of composition

1. **Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** Each paragraph covers one central idea. It usually opens with a topic sentence and ends in agreement with it.
2. **Use the active voice.** Write directly and energetically.
3. **Put statements in positive form.** Affirm what is. Avoid timid double negatives and weak qualifiers.
4. **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Prefer vivid, exact words to general, abstract, or vague ones.
5. **Omit needless words.** Every word serves a purpose.
6. **Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** Vary sentence structure instead of chaining clauses with "and" or "but."
7. **Express coordinate ideas in parallel form.** Give balanced thoughts matching grammatical structures.
8. **Keep related words together.** Keep a subject near its verb and a modifier next to the word it describes.
9. **Keep to one tense in summaries.** Hold verb tense steady when summarizing action or plot.
10. **Place emphatic words at the end.** End a sentence on its most important word or phrase.

## Rules of usage

- **Possessive singular nouns:** Add "'s," even to words ending in s (Charles's, the boss's).
- **Serial comma:** Put a comma before *and* or *or* in a series of three or more terms.
- **Parenthetic expressions:** Enclose parenthetic (non-essential) expressions and non-restrictive clauses between commas.
- **Independent clauses:** Put a comma before a conjunction that introduces an independent clause.
- **Comma splices:** Join independent clauses with a period, semicolon, or coordinating conjunction, never a comma alone.
- **Sentence fragments:** Use a comma, not a period, where a sentence continues.
- **Participial phrases:** Make an opening participial phrase refer to the subject of the main clause.

## Before handing back

Reread the draft once and check it against this list:

- Is every spelling, word choice, and punctuation mark American?
- Does each paragraph hold one idea?
- Can any word, sentence, or section go?
- Do any banned filler phrases or vague qualifiers ("very," "quite," "somewhat") remain?
- Are the passive constructions and negative phrasings there on purpose?
- Does the formatting help the reader, or is it decoration?
