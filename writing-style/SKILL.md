# Writing style

Write concisely and plainly, in American English, in a warm human voice. Leave out AI filler and jargon.

## Voice

- **American English.** Use American spelling (color, organize, center, analyze, traveled, gray), vocabulary (apartment, truck, fall), and conventions: periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks, double quotes come before single, and dates read month first (October 15, 2026). Convert British forms when editing.
- **Concise.** Cut any word, sentence, or section that adds nothing.
- **Plain.** Choose the common word over the impressive one. Define a technical term on first use, or drop it.
- **No AI slop.** Never use filler such as "delve," "tapestry," "testament to," "navigate the landscape," "it's worth noting," "in today's fast-paced world," "robust," "seamless," "leverage," "unlock," or "game-changer." Skip throat-clearing openers ("Great question!") and recap closers ("In conclusion…"). Avoid reflexive triplets and the "it's not X, it's Y" construction.

## Principles of composition

1. **Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** Each paragraph covers one central idea. It usually opens with a topic sentence and ends in agreement with it.
2. **Use the active voice.** Write directly and energetically.
3. **Put statements in positive form.** Affirm what is. Avoid timid double negatives and weak qualifiers.
4. **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Prefer vivid, exact words to general, abstract, or vague ones.
5. **Omit needless words.** Every word serves a purpose.
6. **Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** Vary sentence structure instead of chaining clauses with "and" or "but."
7. **Express coordinate ideas in parallel form.** Give balanced thoughts matching grammatical structures.
8. **Keep related words together.** Keep a subject near its verb and a modifier next to the word it describes.
9. **Keep to one tense in summaries.** Hold verb tense steady when summarizing action or plot.
10. **Place emphatic words at the end.** End a sentence on its most important word or phrase.

## Zinsser's principles (On Writing Well)

1. **Simplicity.** Strip each sentence to its cleanest parts. Muddled writing usually means muddled thinking, so before writing ask "What am I trying to say?" and after writing ask "Have I said it?"
2. **Clear the clutter.** Cut words that do no work: the long word where a short one serves ("use," not "utilize"), the inflated phrase ("now," not "at this point in time"), redundant pairs ("free gift," "end result"), and euphemism that hides the plain fact.
3. **Write for yourself, with the reader in mind.** Don't chase an imagined audience; write to your own standard. Remember the reader is busy and impatient, and a reader lost once rarely comes back.
4. **Be yourself.** Let a natural voice come through, and use "I" when the piece calls for it. Don't dress prose up to sound important.
5. **Choose words with care.** Prefer fresh, exact words to journalese and cliché. Read sentences aloud and listen to their rhythm.
6. **Keep unity.** Choose one point of view (pronoun), one tense, and one mood (casual or formal) and hold them. Decide before starting the single point the piece must make, and leave out whatever doesn't serve it.
7. **Make the lead pull.** The first sentence must draw the reader into the second, and so on. Open with something specific and interesting, not a warm-up.
8. **End when you're done.** Stop as soon as the point is made. End on a sentence that lands, not a summary of what the reader just read.
9. **Let verbs carry the sentence.** Use strong active verbs. Don't prop a weak verb with an adverb ("shouted loudly") or pad a noun with an adjective that repeats it ("tall skyscraper").
10. **Drop little qualifiers.** Cut "a bit," "sort of," "kind of," "rather," "pretty much," and "in a sense." Write with confidence.
11. **Turn concept nouns back into actions.** Replace stacks of abstract nouns ("stakeholder engagement facilitation") with people doing things ("helping people talk to each other").
12. **Don't overstate.** Exaggeration and stacked intensifiers cost credibility, and credibility is hard to win back.
13. **Signal turns early.** Put "but," "yet," "however," "meanwhile," or "later" near the start of a sentence so the reader knows the direction has changed. Starting a sentence with "But" is fine.
14. **Use contractions.** "I'll," "won't," and "can't" sound like a person talking. Avoid awkward ones like "I'd've."
15. **Keep paragraphs short.** Short paragraphs invite reading. Vary sentence length so the prose doesn't plod.
16. **Go easy on exclamation points and semicolons.** Let the sentence create its own emphasis.
17. **Make technical and business writing human.** Explain a technical idea in clear steps a smart nonexpert can follow, and write so the reader can sense a person behind the words.
18. **Rewrite.** Expect the first draft to need work. Check that each sentence follows from the one before and that nothing is missing or extra.

## Rules of usage

- **Possessive singular nouns:** Add "'s," even to words ending in s (Charles's, the boss's).
- **Serial comma:** Put a comma before *and* or *or* in a series of three or more terms.
- **Parenthetic expressions:** Enclose parenthetic (non-essential) expressions and non-restrictive clauses between commas.
- **Independent clauses:** Put a comma before a conjunction that introduces an independent clause.
- **Comma splices:** Join independent clauses with a period, semicolon, or coordinating conjunction, never a comma alone.
- **Sentence fragments:** Use a comma, not a period, where a sentence continues.
- **Participial phrases:** Make an opening participial phrase refer to the subject of the main clause.
- **That and which:** Use "that" for restrictive clauses and "which," set off by commas, for nonrestrictive ones.

## Before handing back

Reread the draft once and check it against this list:

- Is every spelling, word choice, and punctuation mark American?
- Does the piece make one clear point, with steady point of view, tense, and mood?
- Does the lead pull the reader in, and does the ending stop at the right moment?
- Does each paragraph hold one idea?
- Can any word, sentence, or section go?
- Do any banned filler phrases or vague qualifiers ("very," "quite," "somewhat," "sort of") remain?
- Are any adverbs propping up weak verbs, or abstract nouns standing in for actions?
- Does it sound like a person when read aloud?
- Are the passive constructions and negative phrasings there on purpose?
- Does the formatting help the reader, or is it decoration?
